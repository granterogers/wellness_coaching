# Client language (from research interviews)

Status: first pass, 5 interviews (September–November 2026). Mixed group: men and women, roughly 40s–late 50s, in demanding careers, self-employed, between jobs, or full-time parenting.

Public file, so confidentiality rules apply: no names, employers, locations, ages tied to a person, or health conditions tied to a person. Phrases are short and unattributed. Auto-transcripts were noisy, so only clearly captured phrases are quoted; the rest is paraphrased.

## Themes

1. **Sleep is the most common gap.** It came up in 3 of 5 interviews: a mind that won't switch off, waking up repeatedly, tiredness through the day, irritability. People had tried the obvious fixes (less caffeine, no late water or food, less phone) with inconsistent results.
2. **Strength and body as identity.** Wanting to feel strong, capable and grounded in the body; losing belly fat and "getting in shape again"; for some men, strength and sex drive tied to feeling like a man.
3. **Ageing well.** Wanting to keep performing as they get older. One person's role model was a parent still active at 89.
4. **What breaks routines is life, not ignorance.** Family, kids, relationship breakdowns, work emergencies, arriving home exhausted, the phone. Also going "500%" and burning out, perfectionism, and expecting fast results.
5. **What makes habits stick.** Tiny daily commitments (10 minutes a day, a daily push-up target), fitting practice into 5–10 minute pockets, preparing the night before, and getting through the first week. The aim is for it to become automatic, "like brushing your teeth".
6. **What they'd pay a person for.** Two-way conversation rather than videos, apps or AI. Weekly check-ins; daily felt like too much for most. Open, challenging questions. Someone who understands their mental blocks, their personality and their life stage. A perspective they can't reach alone. Rapport ("you'd just know when you find them").
7. **Trust.** Personal experience, recommendations from friends, and people who visibly live what they teach. Science is mainly a filter for hype, not a reason to buy.
8. **Data is divisive.** Some like baseline data for a while, some distrust device accuracy, some think tracking creates anxiety. Measurement should be optional and light.
9. **What they've paid for.** Personal trainers, gym memberships, a sleep/fitness wearable, couples counselling, a chiropractor, a boxing coach.

## Phrases people used (verbatim, unattributed)

- "your brain is still running a million miles"
- "it's just tiredness throughout the day"
- "listen to your body and not to the machines around you"
- "if you do the same thing every day, you do not get the same result"
- "I like to perform like a young guy"
- "I want to become like my mom"
- "Don't think, just do it"
- "not to go too far, 500% into it"
- "I'm in it to win it"
- "open, confronting questions"
- "daily would be too much… monthly might not be enough"
- "everybody should have a coach"
- "lighter in your body"
- "it's not like a computer or an app"
- "a little too good to be true"

## What they want: destination language

- Waking up refreshed, clear-headed, lighter in the body
- Feeling strong, capable, grounded, "a real man" (for some men)
- Still performing well as they get older; staying active into old age
- Health that fits into family and work life instead of competing with it
- Feeling proud of themselves

## Objections and doubts

- Anything that feels like a sales pitch. One interviewee said the end of the interview felt like a sales call and recommended being upfront about intent from the start.
- Claims of fast results ("get fit in four weeks").
- Too many wearables and supplements to choose between, each pushed by someone selling it.
- Over-analysis: data that makes you worry or feel tired.
- Not needing a coach when you're already disciplined.

## Words they did NOT use

Nervous system, regulation, holistic, optimise, biohacking, evidence-based, protocol, journey, vitality, mindfulness. Use plain words: sleep, tired, strong, energy, stress, switch off, stick with it, get older.

## Implications for copy and design

- Lead with the visitor's problem and outcome in their own words (sleep, energy, strength, staying capable with age, making it stick around real life), not with science.
- "Scientist turned practitioner" works as **reassurance**: no hype, no fads, reasons explained, cutting through the noise. It shouldn't be the headline promise. Avoid measurement-led headlines such as "Calm you can measure".
- Embodiment matters. Photos must show Grant living the practice.
- Data is optional and light ("we can track it if you like; you don't have to").
- Sell the relationship: weekly human check-ins, honest challenging questions, built around your life stage.
- Small and doable: 5–10 minute practices that survive a bad week.
- No-pressure, transparent calls to action: say plainly what the conversation is for.
